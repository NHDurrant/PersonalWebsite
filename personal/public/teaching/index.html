Recommended Textbooks + Extra Resources

There are plenty strong textbooks on Real Analysis that will suit any extra revision outside of the provided lecture notes and exercise sheets. I recommend that students search for these themselves such that they get a better feel for what kind of textbook works best for them. This will be helpful as one progresses through one’s degree.

A textbook that I personally found useful was Bartle and Sherbert’s Introduction to Real Analysis. Indeed, for Warwick students, this also includes some overlap with content covered in Sets and Numbers. For Warwick students, the library link is: Introduction to Real Analysis

If you are struggling with building intuition for Analysis, may I suggest Laura Alcock’s book How To Think About Analysis. The Warwick library link is: How To Think About Analysis

I cannot comment on the quality myself, but recently MIT released a full lecture series on Real Analysis onto YouTube, that is quite closely connected to ours at Warwick. I would be careful following other lecture series (this goes for other sets of lecture notes as well) since you don’t want to confuse yourself with different conventions. That said, it is sometimes useful to hear a different perspective and the playlist can be found here.

Advice For Revision Over Christmas

The Analysis I exam at Warwick will be at the start of term 2. In other words: A well earned Christmas break becomes a time to be doing revision. Not such a break after all!

Sadly, one cannot leave the revision to the last minute. Analysis requires careful memorisation of definitions and quick recall of proof/problem solving strategies. Although many will say you shouldn’t have to, memorisation of seen proofs also goes a long way in securing those ‘bookwork’ marks.

I would generally advise to lay out your revision in the following way:

  1. Starting back in Week 1, learn all the definitions that will be applicable to the first problem sheet. Then slowly work through the proofs and ensure you understand each step (no memorisation yet).
  2. Complete the problem sheet, making note of anything you use repeatedly (therefore definitely should remember!). Try to keep the answers as simple as possible – the simpler the better in an exam!
  3. Continue onto the next sheet (learn the next subset of the definitions, understand the proofs), and so on until they are all done.
  4. Attempt a past paper (Warwick students can find these here in a open-book fashion, many with worked solutions) to estimate your current level. Focus on seeing how the results from the course are being examined and used to prove unseen questions.
  5. Work on your weak points then attempt another past paper. Keep repeating this process, trying to use less of your resources each time. Make sure you save at least one paper (usually the most recent) to be done fully under exam conditions.
  6. If time permits, try to memorise each proof in the lecture notes as it is written there. This saves trying to work them out on the spot.
  7. Go smash the exam!

This is only a very general plan, and one should freely change it to suit oneself.

You may be concerned about how much memorisation is now required in preparing for a Mathematics exam, compared to the pre-university level. My only advice for this is from personal experience, and is that I find the flashcard app Anki very useful. Such brute force memorisation is not for everyone, however.
